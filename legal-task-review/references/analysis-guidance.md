# Law task review: analysis-stage guidance

Working reference incorporates the 21-23 October 2026 project clarifications
and the user's 27 October additions on material volume and estimated work
hours, plus the onboarding commentary supplied by the user on 1 November.
This reference covers the assessment stage only. These additions change the
assessment criteria within the existing answer structure, not its fields,
headings, rating options, or recommendation options.

## Current review standard

Evaluate the proposed legal task, its matter materials, reference work, and
grading rubric. The current expected handling time is 1 to 1.5 hours per task.
Focus on representative evidence and high-impact concerns. Do not complete the
legal assignment itself.

## Purpose and scope

Decide whether the request represents work a qualified lawyer would perform,
whether the client situation and supplied documents are coherent, whether the
reference is usable, and whether the rubric would assess an appropriate answer
fairly. This is an evaluation-materials review, not a substantive legal
deliverable or final inclusion approval.

Prioritize whether the synthetic assignment could be assigned and performed
in practice. Assess the reference's practical usability and sample material
defects that affect it; do not independently solve the assignment or audit
every legal conclusion for correctness.

## Review sequence

1. Identify the request: client objective, counsel's role, requested outputs,
   scope, governing jurisdiction, relevant dates, and stated research cutoff.
2. Inspect the materials: read the most relevant matter files and material
   portions of the reference. Skim supporting files and look for missing or
   inconsistent core information.
3. Test the grading rubric: compare coverage and criteria with the assignment.
   Spot-check what would be rewarded or unfairly penalized. Do not answer the
   rubric's substantive questions.
4. Form the assessment: apply ratings to each dimension and give an
   evidence-linked inclusion recommendation.

## Four assessment dimensions

- Legal workflow: Would a lawyer do this work in practice? Are the role,
  required judgment, scope, sequence, and deliverables appropriate?
- Matter realism: Are the client situation, facts, documents, assumptions, and
  dates credible and mutually consistent? Is key information sufficient, and is
  the document pack relevant and proportionate to the assignment?
- Reference work: Does the reference respond to the request with practical,
  usable legal work? Are important issues missing or apparently wrong?
- Grading rubric: Does it reward what matters, match the instructions, apply
  coherent criteria, and accept defensible alternative solutions?

## Firm workflow, briefing, and delegation

A realistic assignment identifies its objective, scope, deliverable,
jurisdiction, and baseline date. Consider what a supervising partner would
normally explain about the matter, client, audience, and purpose. Note a
material gap in that context even when a conditional response remains
possible; distinguish limited briefing from an assignment that cannot be
performed. Do not manufacture missing context where supplied materials or
the lawyer's stated involvement reasonably provide it.

Ask whether a firm would delegate this work to the stated associate or
research assistant. Use a three-year associate as the default benchmark when
no different seniority is specified. Scrutinize demands for a definitive
prediction of an ongoing case's outcome: identify whether a realistic ask
would instead review the procedural position, assess risks, or draft an
appropriate motion. Context matters; a properly scoped litigation-risk or
prospects assessment is not inherently unrealistic.

Compare the active effort needed with the stated deadline and available
staffing. A deadline that plainly cannot accommodate the required work is a
major workflow concern. Explain the mismatch and suggest a realistic deadline
or narrower scope. Base the hours estimate on the actual assignment rather
than copying an illustrative estimate from the onboarding discussion.

Apply a moderately stricter threshold to Legal workflow. A plausible legal
subject and a possible conditional answer do not alone justify Looks sound.
Require a supported basis that a supervising partner could delegate the brief
as written to the stated lawyer and use the requested output for its audience
and stage. Assess briefing, seniority, scope, sequence and prerequisites,
required certainty, and time/resources together. Do not fill critical gaps by
assuming unstated specialist capacity, unlimited overtime, or substantial
partner repair.

Use Minor concerns for a concrete briefing or delegation gap that needs
clarification or narrowing before normal assignment, and Major concerns when
the mismatch materially undermines feasibility or the requested product's
practical use. A conditional response does not cure a mismatch between the
final decision or document demanded and the work that can responsibly be
delegated; expressly preliminary or conditional risk advice can still be
realistic. Do not impose a formal briefing checklist, downgrade a task merely
for complexity, or infer a delivery deadline from the legal cutoff alone.

In the Legal workflow comment, retain the formal, evidence-based analysis and
include an explicitly framed professional judgment, for example, "In my
assessment" or "I would regard this as." For inferences about what an associate
would recognize or how a firm would usually handle the assignment, use
calibrated wording such as "would likely," "most likely," or "would ordinarily"
instead of presenting professional expectations as certain outcomes. Keep
confirmed facts and legal requirements direct; do not add unsupported personal
experience, weaken a supported rating, or hedge every sentence. Make this
judgment within the existing 1 to 3 sentences, not in a new field or section.

For example: "In my assessment, a three-year associate would likely recognize
the as-of date and tentative shipment target as limits on the advice, identify
missing technical findings, and avoid promising clearance." Use such wording
only where the record supports that assessment; this is a voice example, not a
required conclusion or a presumption that every delegation is realistic.

## Ratings: judge impact, not issue count

- Looks sound: No material concern emerged from the portions actually
  inspected. This does not represent exhaustive verification.
- Minor concerns: A stylistic or localized, fixable issue does not substantially
  impair the task, reference, or grading. Prompt wording alone is generally
  minor unless it changes the work or makes the instructions unusable.
- Major concerns: A defect materially affects the realism or feasibility of the
  work, reference reliability, or grading fairness. Examples include work that
  would not be assigned or used in practice, a plainly infeasible deadline,
  or a format failure that defeats the work product's purpose. Explain the
  practical failure; unusual style or personal preference alone is not major.
- Not assessed: Time, available information, or relevant expertise does not
  support a reliable judgment. State what is missing.

A concern should identify the file, passage, assumption, or criterion, explain
its concrete effect, and suggest a targeted correction. Distinguish a confirmed
defect from a suspicion or unresolved uncertainty.

## Rubric quality

Test structure as well as content:

- Coverage: Are central legal issues, reasoning, practical outputs, and
  applicable constraints assessed? Are material elements omitted?
- Relevance: Does each criterion follow from the assignment? Does it demand
  unrelated work or apply unstated requirements?
- Excessive criteria: Do redundant or overly granular checks double-count the
  same issue or distort the assessment?
- Stacked conditions: Does one all-or-nothing criterion bundle independent
  tasks so a narrow omission attracts an unfair penalty? Are its conditions
  necessary, mutually compatible, and aligned with the requested work?
- Logical consistency: Do criteria contradict one another, the stated facts,
  the governing legal framework, or the assignment?
- Alternative approaches: Could a legally correct and practical method receive
  credit when it differs from the reference?
- Proportionality: Does the rubric distinguish serious legal errors from style
  preferences or incidental formatting differences?

Assess whether rubric questions and conditions are appropriate. Criterion
counts such as 45 or 152 in the onboarding discussion illustrate a concern
about proportionality; they are not acceptance thresholds. Where granular
criteria obscure the main objectives, propose grouping them into meaningful
categories. The suggestion of roughly four categories is an example, not a
required rubric design or a change to this review form.

Check whether an answer could satisfy the criteria yet remain unusable to a
partner or client because an essential feature is untested. Inspect enough
examples to identify significant defects in coverage or fairness without
answering every substantive rubric question.

## Material inconsistencies and scenario facts

Accept the stated filing status and applicability of the supplied law and
jurisdiction as working assumptions. Do not routinely verify docket entries,
filing mechanics, or every choice of governing law. Revisit these assumptions
when an obvious, material mismatch undermines a section or the work as a
whole, and identify the conflicting sources.

A wrong jurisdiction, conflicting date, missing essential document, or
unexplained factual contradiction can undermine Matter realism. First check
whether the item is intentional material for the legal task. Errors in a pro se
complaint may be facts the assigned lawyer must analyze, while an unexplained
jurisdiction switch across source files may be a packaging defect.

Apply the jurisdiction, governing date, and research cutoff stated in the task.
Do not flag a legal development after the stated cutoff as a defect. When
controlling context remains unclear, record the uncertainty instead of doing
open-ended investigation.

## Reference and document-pack review

- Responsive work: Does the reference provide what the assignment asks for in a
  form a lawyer could reasonably use or review?
- Legal soundness: Spot-check consequential propositions, reasoning, and
  conclusions for material problems affecting usability. Flag clear errors or
  omissions and label unverified concerns; do not recreate a complete answer.
- Practical usability: Are its assumptions, structure, calculations, and cited
  support intelligible enough to evaluate?
- Document sufficiency: Do the materials contain what the task needs? Is an
  essential source missing, misleading, or inconsistent? Where the assignment
  depends on operative filings or instruments, assess whether summaries or
  emails alone can support it and name any missing primary document.
- Ongoing matters: The task may presuppose knowledge held by an associate
  already involved in the transaction or dispute. This can explain a large or
  context-heavy document pack.

## Work-product structure, style, and format

Judge the requested deliverable and reference against their actual document
type, audience, and purpose. A partner or client should be able to use the
product at the requested stage of work. Do not accept an unexplained rough
draft where a finished product is requested, or demand a final client document
where the assignment expressly seeks a preliminary internal draft.

Check that essential sections are present and ordered conventionally for the
document type. If an unusual structure creates a practical problem, identify
the affected sections, explain the consequence, and propose a workable layout.
Allow defensible variations rather than imposing one preferred house style.

Assess professional legal register in the work product. Distinguish awkward
phrasing in the prompt from language that makes the actual deliverable
unprofessional or unsuitable for its audience. Tie severity to the practical
effect, not the mere presence of stylistic oddities.

In legal memoranda, treat unusually compressed operational shorthand as a
reliability concern, not merely an editing preference. Unexplained codes,
slash-packed commands, and telegraphic conclusions are unreliable when the
reader must reconstruct who acts, what may proceed, the conditions and
exceptions, or the limits of a hold or prohibition. Do not treat a materially
ambiguous instruction as sound simply because its intended meaning can be
inferred elsewhere. Defined abbreviations and concise tables remain acceptable
when the advice is unambiguous. Use Minor concerns for localized compression
that can be repaired without changing material advice; use Major concerns when
it makes a consequential recommendation unsafe to use or requires substantial
reconstruction. Identify the passage, practical effect, and needed rewrite.
Assess this under Reference answer / work product; change Legal workflow only
if the assignment itself creates a distinct workflow problem.

Where a contract redline is requested, check that changes are targeted and
shown as tracked changes or the specified comparison format. Replacing the
whole document so that everything appears changed defeats a targeted redline
and is ordinarily a major reference-work concern. Cite the file and affected
clauses or extent of replacement.

Assess compound deliverables as working documents. A counsel transmittal with
an embedded concise gap chart can be plausible if the parts serve the same
purpose and remain usable. Explain any actual mismatch in function, audience,
or presentation instead of flagging the hybrid format by itself.

If a requested motion or other document has no plausible filing or practical
use in the stated matter, explain that defect under Legal workflow. Distinguish
a locally repairable choice of document or scope from a fundamentally
unrealistic task that warrants Unsuitable. A rubric pass does not cure an
unusable work product.

## Relevance and volume of matter materials

For a large or potentially excessive document pack, assess whether a lawyer
would realistically receive it for this assignment and whether its contents
help produce the requested work. Use the manifest or inventory to establish
the number and types of matter files, then inspect representative groups for
relevance, duplication, and unnecessary versions. Distinguish matter materials
from reference-answer and rubric files when describing volume.

Consider the breadth of the assignment, the lawyer's existing involvement,
the need for triage, and whether an index or document organization makes the
pack usable. Background context, conflicting evidence, or a need to select
relevant documents can be realistic. A high file count alone does not justify
a concern or establish that every file must be read in full.

Include a brief volume assessment in the Matter realism comment for these
packs, even when the volume appears justified. Explain the connection to the
assignment using inspected examples or document groups. If material appears
irrelevant, duplicative, or disproportionate, identify it, explain the effect
on locating key facts or completing the work, and suggest a targeted change
such as pruning duplicates, adding an index, or clarifying the expected scope
of document review. State sampling limits when relevance remains uncertain;
do not claim every file was checked or is necessary from filenames alone.

## Time management for large matters

The 21 October clarification sets a 1 to 1.5 hour review window and supersedes
the older 20 to 30 minute and up-to-two-hour references.

Review the final request closely, examine key documents, and spot-check a sample
of the reference and rubric for high-impact concerns. A pack of 67 to 80 or more
files, or a long rubric, does not call for verifying every spreadsheet cell or
researching every issue to conclusion.

Use Not assessed when a dimension cannot be responsibly assessed within the
available evidence and time. State the limit. Do not imply that the entire file
set or reference was verified when only a sample was examined.

## Difficulty and lawyer-completion hours

If a separate form requests difficulty, use:

- Straightforward: Routine work with limited judgment or synthesis.
- Moderate: Some substantive judgment, synthesis, or organization is required.
- Difficult: Substantial legal complexity, research, or coordination is needed.
- Very difficult: Unusually demanding analysis, volume, or interdependent legal
  issues.
- Cannot estimate: The evidence does not support a meaningful judgment.

The current review ends with Estimated work hours. Estimate active time for a
lawyer familiar with the practice area to read and triage the supplied
materials, research, draft, and check the requested assignment. Exclude this
review and waiting for outside information. Include realistic document
selection and review effort without assuming every file needs the same depth
of reading. Base the estimate on scope, material volume, complexity, and the
required deliverables; use a single number of hours, allowing decimals where
appropriate. Use `Cannot estimate` if missing inputs prevent a reasonable
estimate. Use realistic associate effort for the stated role and compare it
with any deadline under Legal workflow. This is separate from the reviewer's
1 to 1.5 hour window. Difficulty is not a task-quality rating.

## Inclusion recommendation

The Guidance uses these broad categories:

- Promising: The task appears worth retaining for further evaluation based on
  the materials inspected.
- Needs revision: The task is potentially useful but identifiable issues need
  correction.
- Unsuitable: Fundamental problems make it a poor evaluation candidate.
- Insufficient information: The available basis does not support a defensible
  inclusion judgment.

The requested output splits Needs revision into Minor revision for local edits
and Major revision for substantial repair or redesign.

Explain the recommendation in 1 to 3 sentences. Connect it to the highest-impact
finding and a realistic change where relevant. Keep the recommendation
consistent with the four dimension ratings.

## Concise, evidence-based comments

Aim for 1 to 3 sentences per dimension. Use this structure:

1. Evidence: identify the relevant assignment provision, file, passage,
   calculation, assumption, or rubric criterion.
2. Impact: explain the likely effect on realism, usability, or fair grading.
3. Correction: suggest a change proportionate to the issue. If the point remains
   unverified, say so.

Identify exact files, clauses, sections, or criterion IDs when available. For
example, compare a monetary requirement with the cap in the identified
controlling approval and explain the conflict. For a structural defect, name
the problematic section order and propose a usable alternative. Generic
comments such as "looks fine" or "structure is odd" do not establish a finding;
leave an optional comment empty when there is nothing substantive to add.

## Final consistency check

- Does the assessment reflect the actual request, jurisdiction, legal cutoff,
  scope, baseline date, and outputs?
- Would a firm delegate this work to the stated role, with enough partner
  context and a deadline consistent with realistic active effort?
- Are the work product's structure, register, format, and compound components
  appropriate for its document type and intended audience?
- Are the four ratings grounded in inspected material, with Not assessed used
  when needed?
- Was the rubric assessed for coverage, stacked criteria, contradictions, and
  alternative valid approaches without answering it? Could an unusable product
  still pass it?
- Are concerns tied to concrete evidence and practical consequences?
- For a large document pack, does Matter realism address relevance, necessity,
  and proportionality using inspected evidence and acknowledge sampling limits?
- Is Estimated work hours present at the end, with active lawyer time kept
  separate from reviewer handling time and waiting for outside information?
- Is the recommendation short, proportionate, and consistent with the ratings?
